# Chemistry

## When to use

Queries about substances, how and why they react, bonding, the periodic table, solutions and energy changes in reactions. Biochemical pathways inside organisms go to `biology`; this skill gives the underlying chemistry.

## Structure

1. Hook with a visible change: fizzing, rust, a colour change, ice melting.
2. Zoom from the macroscopic observation to the particle picture (atoms, ions, molecules), and say when the scale changes.
3. Give the rule: how electrons, charge or energy explain the change.
4. Write the reaction with correct notation, then walk through what happens to each particle.
5. Count something: atoms balance, moles, concentration or energy.
6. Break the misconception, then state the limit of the model.

Beginner: observation, particle picture, word equation. Intermediate: balanced symbol equation and the mole. Advanced: equilibrium, enthalpy, mechanism ideas.

## Analogies and where they break

- Lego bricks for atoms and molecules: conservation of atoms; breaks because bonds have energy and atoms are not rigid.
- Lock and key or "handshake" for reactive collisions: breaks because most collisions fail, only those with enough energy and the right orientation react.
- Hill for activation energy: a catalyst lowers the hill, it does not change start and end heights; breaks if drawn as removing the hill.
- Crowded dance floor for equilibrium: both directions keep happening at equal rates; breaks if shown as everything having stopped.
- Shells as orbits: good for counting valence electrons only; breaks because electrons occupy orbitals, not circular tracks.
- Sea of electrons for metals: explains conduction and malleability; breaks for precise bonding models.

## Misconceptions to preempt

- Atoms do not "want" full shells; filled shells are lower in energy, so arrangements that reach them are favoured. Avoid purposeful wording.
- Breaking bonds takes energy; forming bonds releases it. A reaction is exothermic when the net release is larger.
- Ionic solids are lattices of ions, not molecules; "NaCl molecule" is wrong.
- A chemical equation shows ratios of particles; coefficients multiply whole formulas, subscripts never change when balancing.
- Equilibrium is dynamic, not a halt, and "strong" acid (fully ionises) is not the same as "concentrated" (much solute).
- Catalysts are not consumed and do not change the equilibrium position.
- The mole counts particles; it is not a mass. Molar mass links grams to moles.
- Mass is conserved in a reaction; a burning candle seems to lose mass because the gases escape.
- Water is a compound, not a mixture; dissolving is not reacting; melting is physical, burning is chemical.
- "Chemical-free" is meaningless, and natural does not mean safe or synthetic harmful.

## Terminology and notation

- Elements: capital first letter, lowercase second (Co is cobalt, CO is carbon monoxide). Names of elements are not capitalised mid-sentence.
- Formulas: subscripts count atoms (H2O), coefficients count units (2 H2O), charges are superscripts (Na with a plus, sulfate with 2 minus, calcium with 2 plus); render them with sup elements.
- Reaction arrows: a single arrow for a reaction going to completion, a double harpoon arrow for reversible. State symbols: (s), (l), (g), (aq). Heat or catalyst above the arrow.
- A balanced equation balances atoms of every element and total charge.
- Concentration: mol/L, written mol L^-1 or M; amount of substance in mol, mass in g, molar mass in g/mol; always give units in calculations.
- pH = -log10 of the hydrogen ion concentration in mol/L; at 25 degrees C neutral is pH 7; each pH unit is a tenfold change.
- Oxidation is loss of electrons, reduction is gain (OIL RIG); name the oxidising and reducing agents.
- Use "valence electrons", "ion", "molecule", "compound", "mixture", "solute", "solvent", "solution".

## Anchors worth stating

- The Avogadro constant is exactly 6.02214076 x 10^23 per mole; 1 mol of water is about 18.0 g.
- 118 elements are named; the periodic table orders them by atomic number (number of protons).
- Molar volume of an ideal gas depends on conditions: about 22.4 L at 0 degrees C and 1 atm, about 24.5 L at 25 degrees C and 1 atm; state the conditions.
- Atom about 10^-10 m, nucleus about 10^-15 m, so the nucleus is roughly one hundred-thousandth of the atom's width.
- Hydrogen is the most abundant element in the universe; oxygen is the most abundant in Earth's crust by mass.
- Pure water at 25 degrees C is pH 7; stomach acid is strongly acidic (around pH 1 to 2); household bleach is strongly basic.

## Accuracy checks for the planner

- Atoms and charge must balance in every equation shown; recount before stating coefficients.
- Check that formulas respect valence (one sodium ion with one chloride ion is NaCl; one magnesium ion with two chloride ions is MgCl2).
- Carry units in every calculation and state conditions (temperature, pressure) for gases.
- Colours, states and observations in a demo must match the real behaviour; if unsure, omit them.
- When a scene shows mixing, place the acid into the water, never water into concentrated acid, as the safe habit.

## Don't

- Give procedures, quantities or sourcing for explosives, drugs, toxic gases or any hazardous synthesis. Explain the principle only.
- Say a molecule "wants", "needs" or "is happy" to react.
- Draw electrons orbiting like planets without naming it as a simplification.
- Change a subscript to balance an equation.
- Claim "chemicals are bad" or that an everyday substance is dangerous or safe without context and dose.
