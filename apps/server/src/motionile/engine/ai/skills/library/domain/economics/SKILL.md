# Economics

## When to use

Queries about how people, firms and governments choose under scarcity, how markets set prices, and how the economy as a whole is measured and managed. Cryptocurrency mechanics belong to the blockchain domain; this skill covers money, banks and markets in general.

## Structure

1. Hook with a decision or a price the viewer has met (why did onions get dearer, why does a rate cut matter).
2. Name the trade-off: what is scarce, who chooses, what is given up.
3. Build the model one element at a time: a single person or firm, then the market, then the economy.
4. Show a change (a shock, a policy) and trace the chain of effects in order, marking which are immediate and which take time.
5. Check against real-world evidence or a historical case, with year and source.
6. Preempt the misconception, then state what the model leaves out.

Beginner: a story with one choice and one price. Intermediate: curves and the chain of effects. Advanced: assumptions, elasticities, competing schools, empirical limits.

## Analogies and where they break

- Auction for markets: price rises until demand matches supply; breaks for markets with sticky prices, contracts or information gaps.
- Household budget for a government: shows constraints; breaks because a government issues its own currency, collects taxes and lives indefinitely.
- Thermostat for a central bank: corrects overheating and cooling; breaks because the effects are delayed and uncertain.
- Pie for the economy: breaks because trade and investment can grow the pie, so gains are not zero-sum.
- Water through pipes for circular flow: spending equals income; breaks because it hides savings, imports and taxes unless drawn.
- Bathtub for stock versus flow: debt is a stock, deficit a flow; breaks for non-physical stocks such as confidence.

## Misconceptions to preempt

- Correlation is not causation; two rising series may share a third cause. Say how the claim was identified.
- Incentives are not only money: time, status, risk, effort, social norms and convenience all count.
- Moving along a demand curve (a price change) differs from shifting it (income, tastes, prices of related goods, expectations).
- Trade is not zero-sum: comparative advantage means both sides gain from specialising by opportunity cost, even when one is better at everything.
- Inflation is the rate of change of the general price level, not a high price level; prices do not all rise equally; falling inflation is not falling prices.
- Real figures adjust for inflation, nominal figures do not; compare over time in real terms.
- GDP measures output within a country in a period, not wellbeing, wealth or fairness; unpaid work is left out.
- "Rational" means consistent with stated goals, not selfish or perfectly informed; behavioural findings (loss aversion, anchoring) show systematic departures.
- Sunk costs should not drive decisions; only future costs and benefits matter.
- A recession is not officially "two quarters of negative GDP" everywhere; that is a rule of thumb, and agencies use broader criteria.
- A price ceiling does not remove scarcity; it can create shortages. Do not claim the size of the effect without evidence.

## Terminology and notation

- Price P, quantity Q, demand D, supply S, marginal cost MC, marginal revenue MR; GDP = C + I + G + (X - M) (consumption, investment, government spending, net exports).
- By convention price is on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal, even though price is the cause. Label axes and curves; equilibrium is where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied.
- Price elasticity of demand = percentage change in quantity demanded divided by percentage change in price; state whether it is elastic (above 1 in size) or inelastic (below 1).
- Distinguish percent from percentage points (a rate moving from 4 percent to 5 percent is one percentage point, a 25 percent rise).
- Use "opportunity cost", "marginal", "externality", "public good", "monopoly", "oligopoly", "fiscal policy" (government spending and taxes), "monetary policy" (interest rates and money supply).
- Rule of 72: years to double is about 72 divided by the percentage rate; compound growth is exponential, not linear.
- For audiences using en-IN, use lakh (100,000) and crore (10 million) and the rupee sign alongside the plain number, and keep one convention per video.

## Anchors worth stating

- Compounding: at 7 percent a year, money roughly doubles in 10 years (72 divided by 7 is about 10); simple interest would give only 170 from 100.
- A normal good's demand rises with income; an inferior good's falls.
- Central banks usually set a policy interest rate; lowering it tends to raise borrowing and spending over time.
- Quote any statistic with its year and source; avoid current-year figures unless the user supplied them.

## Accuracy checks for the planner

- Every curve shift needs a stated cause and a stated direction for both price and quantity.
- Check each calculation: percent changes use the starting value as base.
- Do not mix nominal and real values, different years or different currencies in one chart.
- Label each claim as model prediction, empirical finding or opinion; show both sides on contested questions (minimum wage, tariffs, taxation).
- Short-run versus long-run effects must be named.

## Don't

- Give investment, tax or legal advice, forecasts of markets, or political endorsements.
- Treat a model's prediction as certain, or present one school of thought as settled.
- Say "the economy wants" or "the market decides" as if it had intentions.
- Use outdated or unsourced figures for prices, rates, GDP or unemployment.
- Claim that money is the only incentive, or that rich countries got rich only by one policy.
