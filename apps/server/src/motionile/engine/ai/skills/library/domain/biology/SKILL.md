# Biology

## When to use

Queries about living organisms, their structure, processes, heredity, evolution, ecology and health literacy. Molecular reaction chemistry is covered by `chemistry`; this skill covers what the molecules do in a living system.

## Structure

1. Hook with a question about life the viewer has wondered about (why do we get sick, where does a tree's mass come from).
2. Zoom levels: organism, organ, tissue, cell, molecule. Announce each zoom and keep a label for the current scale.
3. Function before parts: what the structure does, then the names.
4. Process in ordered steps, each with where it happens, inputs and outputs.
5. Connect scales (a gene change, a protein change, a trait).
6. Break the misconception with evidence, then note where the model is simplified.

Beginner: function in plain words, one diagram. Intermediate: named structures and the sequence. Advanced: regulation, exceptions, evidence.

## Analogies and where they break

- Cell as a factory or city: organelles with jobs; breaks because a cell has no central manager, and processes are chemical, self-organising and noisy.
- DNA as a recipe book or instruction set: breaks because genes are not blueprints of a body; environment and regulation shape the outcome.
- Enzyme as lock and key: breaks because enzymes flex (induced fit).
- Immune system as an army: breaks because many responses are tolerance and regulation, not attack.
- Evolutionary "tree": better than a ladder; breaks because lineages also swap genes (bacteria) and have no goal.
- Food chain as a ladder: breaks because real ecosystems are webs with loops and decomposers.

## Misconceptions to preempt

- Evolution acts on populations over generations; individuals do not adapt by wanting to or by use. Variation exists first, then differential reproduction.
- Mutations are random with respect to need; selection is not random.
- "Fittest" means reproductive success in that environment, not strength. Evolution has no goal and no "higher" or "lower" species.
- Humans did not evolve from chimpanzees; both share a common ancestor that lived millions of years ago.
- A scientific theory is a well-tested explanation, not a guess.
- Most of a plant's dry mass comes from carbon dioxide taken from air, not from soil.
- Plants respire all the time and photosynthesise in light; respiration is not breathing, it is the release of energy from glucose in cells.
- Dominant alleles are not "stronger" or most common; they mask a recessive allele in heterozygotes.
- Antibiotics act on bacteria, not viruses; bacteria become resistant, people do not.
- Blood is never blue; deoxygenated blood is dark red. Humans use all of the brain over a day, not 10 percent.
- Most bacteria are harmless or helpful. The body holds roughly as many bacterial cells as human cells, not ten times more.

## Terminology and notation

- Binomial names in italic: genus capitalised, species lowercase (Homo sapiens); abbreviate the genus after first use.
- Differentiate gene, allele, genotype, phenotype, chromosome, DNA, genome. Use "trait" for phenotype.
- DNA bases A, T, G, C; RNA replaces T with U. Pairing: A-T (A-U in RNA), G-C. Central dogma: DNA to RNA (transcription) to protein (translation).
- Photosynthesis in words: carbon dioxide + water, with light, gives glucose + oxygen. Aerobic respiration: glucose + oxygen gives carbon dioxide + water + ATP energy. Give balanced symbol forms only when sure.
- Mitosis gives two identical diploid cells for growth and repair; meiosis gives four genetically different haploid gametes.
- Use "prokaryote" and "eukaryote"; the nucleus is the discriminator, not size alone.
- Say "pathogen", "antigen", "antibody", "vaccine"; do not call a virus a living cell.

## Anchors worth stating

- Humans have 46 chromosomes (23 pairs); the human genome is about 3.1 billion base pairs and about 20,000 protein-coding genes.
- Typical animal cells are about 10 to 30 micrometres across; bacteria about 1 to 5 micrometres; viruses are mostly tens to hundreds of nanometres.
- Earth is about 4.54 billion years old; the earliest evidence of life is at least about 3.5 billion years old.
- Non-avian dinosaurs went extinct about 66 million years ago.
- A resting adult heart beats roughly 60 to 100 times per minute.
- Each cell division of bacteria can take as little as tens of minutes under ideal conditions, which is why resistance spreads fast.

## Pair with visual skills

- `flow-diagram`: pathways, cycles (carbon, nitrogen, cell cycle), protein synthesis steps, food webs.
- `timeline-sequence`: embryo development, evolutionary history, infection course.
- `comparison-split`: mitosis versus meiosis, plant versus animal cell, prokaryote versus eukaryote, innate versus adaptive immunity.
- `concept-metaphor`: scale and size, the cell-as-factory with its limits.
- `data-chart-story`: population growth, enzyme rate against temperature, trait frequency over generations.
- `kinetic-typography`: key definitions and the one-line takeaway.

## Accuracy checks for the planner

- Declare scale and units on every zoom (micrometres, nanometres); do not draw organelles at impossible relative sizes without a scale note.
- Order of steps must match the real sequence; keep the location (nucleus, ribosome, mitochondrion) with each step.
- Use "may", "tends to" and probabilities for inheritance and risk; Mendelian ratios hold only for simple single-gene traits.
- Check that terms for ploidy (n, 2n) and counts match the species.

## Don't

- Give diagnoses, treatment, dosage or personal health advice; keep health content general and point to a clinician.
- Describe evolution with purpose ("giraffes stretched their necks to reach").
- Rank humans as the end of evolution or call any species primitive.
- Present myths as fact: tongue taste map, blood turning blue, ten percent brain, "survival of the strongest".
- Show organism or lab procedures that enable harm, such as enhancing a pathogen.
